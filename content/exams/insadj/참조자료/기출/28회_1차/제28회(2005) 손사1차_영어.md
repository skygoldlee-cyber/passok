     Old assumptions have been challenged right and left, but it has been difficult to replace them with any new certainties—in fact, the very notion of certainty is alien to the 20th-century cast of mind. Blame the physicists. In 1905 Albert Einstein published his Special Theory of Relativity, in which he argued that time and distance were not absolutes, but could be shorter or longer depending on the relative motion between the observer and the thing observed. In 1927 Werner Heisenberg came up with his Uncertainty Principle, according to which, in subatomic experiments, the very act of observation distorts reality in such a way that one can determine either the position or the velocity of a particle, but never both at once. Reality is loosed from its moorings, and the human observer becomes an agent in determining what's there. At the end of the century, these perceptions had been carried to some wild "postmodern" extremes. In literature, "deconstructionists" held that it was readers, not authors, who create meaning in the text.

     There are several important applications of the principle of expansion of solids due to heating the solid. One application is found in the type of thermometer called a thermocouple. If a strip of metal is heated, it will expand. Some metals will expand more than others for the same amount of heat; each metal has a definite expansion for a specific amount of heat. Therefore, if two different metal strips are bound together and heated, the single strip of metals will tend to assume a curved position because of the greater expansion of one metal as compared to the other. This curvature can make contact with an electrical circuit, starting an electric motor or operating some other electromechanical device. In the home a thermocouple stops or starts the oil burner in accordance with the room temperature. The temperature of a gasoline engine can be measured with a device similar to a thermocouple.

     Cultural anthropologists are scientists who study how members of human societies experience events in their lives. They examine how people in a culture work, find their food, build their shelters, marry, raise their children, care for the ill, and treat their dead. They may find that some cultures have practices unlike those of their own culture.    (  A  ), an American anthropologist discovers that in some cultures it is normal to find insects as part of the human diet. The anthropologist also learns that in ancient cultures, human sacrifice was practiced. Most Americans would find these practices abhorrent.
     If anthropologists were to condemn such behaviors as “wrong,” they would be assuming that their own culture is superior, since it does things the “right” way. But this would lead to the conclusion they should not waste time studying other cultures. If their own way is “right,” and others are “wrong,” they would then merely be studying “mistakes.”    (  B  ), anthropologists try to understand a culture on its own terms. What purpose or meaning do the behaviors have to the people who practice them? The ancient Aztec culture of Mexico believed that the universe was periodically destroyed by the gods, and the only way to prevent this disaster was to sacrifice humans. While this does not mean anthropologists need to approve of human sacrifice, by understanding the role it played in a culture, they can avoid judging it by the society's standards.
